Can I borrow what other people do with their agents?

2026-10-26

Last night I did the same thing twice. Both times I saw a post about what other people do with their agents, and both times my reply was to hand the post to my own bot.

The first was a post by @Crypto_QianXun, written in Chinese. It opens with a complaint I recognise. In my rough translation: the complaint they hear most is that people don't know what to use AI agents for. They set up Grok Bot, Muse, Hark, Dots or Hermes, and then leave them to gather dust.

The fix it suggests is a free site, Agent Use Cases, which collects real examples of what people do with agents. When I opened it, the page said it had 1,606 use cases and was updated Oct 25, pulled from the open web, X, Reddit and Hacker News. Each one has a short description, the agent used, and a prompt you can copy. The post's own advice is to give the link to your agent and let it find the ones that fit you.

So that's what I did:

"study this website, make recommendations of what all can we borrow"

A list of ideas is not the hard part

Scrolling the page, the examples are oddly specific. Someone's bot watches severe weather alerts and emails their family. Someone else runs cron jobs (tasks on a timer) that browse competitor sites for a morning brief. Another person had Claude Code read their bank statements, interview them about goals, and build a retirement plan.

None of these are clever ideas on their own. What makes them work is that each one belongs to a person with that exact problem. The weather bot exists because someone has family overseas. That's why I'm not sure a list of 1,606 ideas fixes the dust problem. Reading other people's chores doesn't tell me which chores are mine.

Still, I think it helps in one way. It shows me what kind of thing is possible, so I notice my own version sooner. "Oh, I also check that every morning by hand."

The second time

The second post was a roundup of Grok Bots that Elon had praised, and my reply was even lazier:

"make each bot give it some task, follow them until it's done so I can see what we got in the morning"

Reading it back, I was asking a bot to try out other bots overnight and report what came of it. I wasn't choosing which ideas to keep. I was asking for a trial run of all of them.

Where the dust actually comes from

That fits with another post I bookmarked, from @tsukiema_. It quotes Lauren Tan from SpaceXAI saying "99% of people use GrokBot for 1% of what it can do," and that she runs "a team of 50+ GrokBot agents" with a chief of staff that plans the day by 8 a.m. That's a quote inside someone else's hype post, so I'm treating it as their summary of her, not her exact words.

But the shape of it is useful. In that picture, nobody sits there thinking up what to ask. The work arrives on its own schedule, and the person mostly checks results. The post's last line is "Anyone can ask GrokBot a question, almost nobody gives it a team to run."

I think that's the real reason agents gather dust. It's not a lack of ideas. It's that a chat box waits for you to come up with something, and most days you don't. The bots of mine that I actually rely on are the ones on a timer. They run every morning whether I remember them or not.

What I'll try

So when my bot comes back with its list of things to borrow, I want to ask one question of each: would I want this to run without me asking? If yes, it becomes a routine. If it only makes sense when I think of it, it probably joins the pile of things I'll forget.

I'm still curious what the overnight trial turns up. But my guess is the winners won't be the cleverest ideas on the list. They'll be the boring ones that match something I already do by hand.
