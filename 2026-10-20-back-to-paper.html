Is AI quietly sending learning back to paper?

2026-10-20

I noticed something odd in this week's bookmarks. Several of the AI learning projects I saved don't end on a screen. They end with a crank you turn, a page you print, or a pen on paper with a camera watching.

A chain of people building toys

It started as a little chain of replies. @wheresryan22 posted a prototype of a kids' version of their explainer, built around one question:

"How does a pump fill a tyre?"

They said they're trying "to find the simplest way to help kids understand how things work, while still keeping enough detail to make the experience engaging, interactive, and educational."

@Aprilzz423 replied with their own take, "learning through play," and a little washing machine. Then they turned it into a skill called Colorfigures: describe an everyday object and Claude draws it "hand-inked and isometric, with buttons that actually work." You can also ask how something works and it labels the parts.

Then @0x_fokki added a pencil sharpener:

"Two cutters orbit the pencil like planets and 12 turns of the crank make the point :)"

Their opening line is the one I keep thinking about: "This is how I wish someone had explained physics to me." I get why. A diagram with arrows tells you what happens, but I suspect twelve turns of a crank lets you feel it.

And then, paper

The same @wheresryan22 later posted that their anatomy figures now print: "You can now generate printable PDFs of any figure," for home, the classroom, or homework.

And @nichochar open sourced Ari. The repo describes it as "Learn stuff with pen and paper + an AI tutor." You point a camera at your page, Ari builds a curriculum with exercises, and it "can always see what you are writing," so you can ask questions as you go and ask it to grade you at the end. In the post that started it he wrote:

"every morning do 45mins of math or physics ... i was so rusty but it's coming back, feels so good to use my brain this way again"

That line got me. The AI isn't doing the thinking there. It's setting up the conditions for him to think with his hands.

Playing with a machine is not the same as working a problem

Here's where I'm unsure. The crank and the washing machine are lovely for seeing how something works. But I suspect I could watch a beautiful animation and forget it by the next day. Ari asks for something different: you do the exercise, and somebody checks it.

My guess is they're two halves of the same thing. The toy gives you the picture, and the paper makes you prove you actually have it. I don't know which half I'm missing more right now. Probably the paper.

It also flips something I've written about twice already. I keep wondering about an agent as a curious student going through my lectures. These projects suggest the other way round: I'm the student, and the agent is the patient tutor who sets the next problem.

Whose memory is it?

One detail in Ari's README connects to another bookmark. It keeps a TUTOR.md for teaching style, a USER.md for what it's learned about you, and a memories/ folder, all saved on your own computer.

On the same day, @mattpocockuk wrote that three months in, his personal wiki "has become critical infrastructure for my business," and:

"Relying on a vendor for memory feels like a recipe for lock-in."

A tutor that knows where I got stuck last week is only useful if it still remembers next year. If that memory lives in plain files I own, I can move it to a different tool or model later. I'm not sure most learning apps will work that way, but it now feels like the first thing I'd check.

So, the small experiment I want to try: a few mornings of actual problems on actual paper, with something watching that can tell me where I went wrong. I'm curious whether it feels like school or like play.
