Who are agent personas actually for?

2026-10-17

I keep giving my agents little identities. One reads my bookmarks, one writes, and a while ago I suggested turning one into a student. This week a short exchange on X made me ask why I do that, and whether it helps anyone but me.

The question

Anthony Morris asked the blunt version:

"I don't want to manage a agents with different personas. If intelligence is truly super there should be no need for roles. Smart generalists rule the world."

And then: "If you're making agents with personas, are you actually finding value from it?"

My first reaction was that he's probably right about the model. A capable model doesn't need to be told it's "the reviewer" to review well. If the persona is doing real work for the agent, that sounds like a crutch that should fade as models get better.

The answer that changed how I see it

@poteto's reply moved the question somewhere else:

"it's not about helping the agent, it's so you have one less thing on your mind you need to consciously think about. personas facilitate type 1 thinking and make it easy for you to context switch as you start to multitask more and more"

I take "type 1 thinking" to mean the fast, automatic kind, the kind you don't have to reason through. So a persona is a label for me. When I open the one that handles bookmarks, I don't have to reload what it does, what it's allowed to touch, or what I told it last week. The name carries that.

That matches my experience better than "it makes the model smarter." Having a few agents with clear jobs feels calmer than one big chat where I have to keep re-explaining which hat it's wearing.

Where I'm less sure

If personas are for the human, then what the persona does still has to be pinned down somewhere else. A name doesn't enforce anything.

I noticed this in my own posts. When I saw a tip about surfacing posts with more bookmarks than likes, I told my bookmark bot to "take this into account for my bookmark algorithm as a further way to check credibility," to just unbookmark skipped posts instead of emailing me about them, and to check whether I'd already bookmarked something similar. Every one of those is a rule I typed as a casual reply. None of it lives in the persona's name. It lives in whatever the bot remembers from that message.

Matt Pocock's line fits here:

"Don't blame the agent for writing the bad test

Blame the codebase for allowing the bad test to be written"

Swap "codebase" for "setup" and I think it applies to personas too. If my bookmark bot emails me about something it should have quietly skipped, the persona didn't fail. The setup let it happen. The persona is how I find the right agent quickly, but the rules still need to be written down somewhere the agent can't misread, and ideally somewhere a check can catch.

The student idea, again

This also changes how I think about the curious student idea I floated: "Turn grokbot into a student with a curious persona," going through my lectures, finding readings, doing its own research.

By Anthony's logic, "curious" shouldn't be needed; a smart model will be as curious as the task requires. By poteto's logic, "curious student" is mostly for me: it tells me what to expect when I check in on it. I think both are partly true. The part that would actually make it a good student isn't the word "curious." It's the concrete stuff: did it read the source, did it cite it, did it connect today's lecture to an earlier one. Those I could check.

So my working answer to Anthony's question: yes, I'm finding value, but the value is in my head, not in the model's output. The name is for me. The rules are for the agent, and they should be written somewhere sturdier than a reply on X.
